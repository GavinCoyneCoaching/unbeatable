The Word That Means Two Different Things

A few years ago, one of my daughters taught me something without either of us realising it at the time. She was doing something small she shouldn't — I said "stop." She stopped. A bit later, with actual danger involved, I raised my voice and shouted "STOP!" She stopped again. Same word. Completely different message. In my head, I understood the difference perfectly — one was about behaviour, one was about safety. But she didn't hear two different things. She just heard "STOP," with extra volume and emotion behind it, and responded the same way to both. Total loss in translation. Kids don't hear your intention. They hear your volume. It's the same on the pitch. You've got a player on the far side, and you're roaring instructions across the grass — because they're far away, not because you're angry. But that's not how it lands. Kids read the volume as the emotion, every time. This is why communication isn't just the words we use — it's the meaning attached to them. And where breakdowns happen is when we get attached to what we said instead of what they actually heard. The goal isn't to change the message. It's to improve the translation.

This week's challenge

Catch one moment this week where volume might get misread as anger — a call across a pitch, a "stop" at home. Try saying it a notch calmer than instinct tells you to, and watch whether it lands differently.
