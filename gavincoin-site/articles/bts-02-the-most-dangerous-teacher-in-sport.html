Beyond the Scoreboard

The Most Dangerous Teacher In Sport

Over the years, as a coach and a parent, I've come to believe something that sounds strange the first time you hear it. Losing isn't the most dangerous teacher in sport. Winning is. Not all winning. Winning for the wrong reasons.

A win doesn't automatically mean everything went well. We've all seen it. A team wins comfortably, so nobody looks any closer. The lazy habits get ignored. The lack of communication gets ignored. The player who drifted through the game gets the same result as the one who worked for everything. And the lesson everyone quietly takes home is: we won, so everything must be fine. A few weeks later, against a better team, all those ignored habits show up at once — and suddenly it's a crisis nobody saw coming. Except it wasn't sudden at all. It just took a loss to reveal what a win had been hiding.

A good result can give you permission to stop asking questions. That's the part that makes it dangerous — not the winning itself, but the silence that follows it. This isn't about being suspicious of winning. Winning matters, and nobody trains all winter hoping to lose. It's about staying honest about how the win happened, so the good habits keep getting reinforced and the bad ones don't get to hide behind a good scoreline.

This week's challenge

The next time your team or your child wins, celebrate it fully — then ask one quiet question: "What actually went well today, and what did we get away with?" The teams who ask that after a win are the ones who don't get caught out later.
