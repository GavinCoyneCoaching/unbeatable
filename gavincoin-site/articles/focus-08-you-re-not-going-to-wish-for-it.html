Focus

You're Not Going to Wish For It

For a long time, I lived a bit too much in the hypothetical. When I have this, then I'll be able to that. When I get that promotion, that resource, that break — then I'll finally be able to focus on building the life I want. The trouble is, life doesn't really work backwards like that. What actually changed things for me was something much simpler: practising gratitude. Not in a vague, wishy-washy way — properly focusing, in the mirror, on what I had and what I could do, right then. And then actually doing those things.

Do the best with what you have. When you have better, do better. That one small shift gave me a platform to actually grow — instead of waiting for a version of my life that had the resources I didn't have yet. My daughter's the same. Before she could score more, she had to improve her kicking. Then her accuracy. Then she had to work on something else entirely — closing the gap between missing a shot and taking the next one. Because sometimes she'd miss, and then there'd be ages before she'd go again. That gap, between a bad moment and the next one — if it's left too long, you just fester and stew in it. It's like leaving a tea bag in too long. It gets so strong and bitter. So you learn to close the gap. You're not going to wish for it. You're going to work for it. That goes for the eight-year-olds too, in a different way. After a blitz, after training, my youngest always asks the same thing — "Daddy, did I win?" Well, first of all, no, because we don't keep score at under-sevens. But more than that — let's set her up on the right pathway from the start. At that age, what actually matters is catching, passing, running, spreading out, blocking. Not "you won, you won, you won." For teenagers, it's a different job — catching them where they are and helping them steer the ship back onto a better track, using everything else we've covered. Either way, the work comes first. Not the wish.

This week's challenge

If your player is fixated on a result they don't have yet, ask them one question instead of reassuring them it'll come: "What's the one thing, right now, you can actually work on?" Then help them do it. Not wish about it. Where is my attention right now?
